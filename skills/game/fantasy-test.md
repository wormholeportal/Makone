# Step 0 — Find a feeling worth being obsessive about

> Read this BEFORE `workflow.md`. Most AI-made games die here, not
> in the code. They die because nobody decided **what the game is FOR** —
> what it wants the player to feel — before deciding what the player does.
>
> Nobody sits down to "make a work of art." They get obsessed with one
> specific feeling and chase it to the bone, and *that* becomes a work.
> Hollow Knight isn't "a metroidvania" — someone was in love with melancholy
> beauty + precise dread, and built everything to serve it. Your job in Step 0
> is to find that one feeling. The game is just the form it finally takes.

## The one thing that separates a work from a tech demo

A tech demo starts from a **form**: "a platformer", "a shooter", "a puzzle
game". You can feel it — the mechanics are correct, the world is decorated,
and it is completely dead inside. There's no reason this game exists rather
than any other game in its genre.

A work starts from a **feeling**: "the ache of finishing something for
someone who's gone" (that's `clockheart`). The mechanic, the palette, the
ending — all of it is downstream of, and in service to, that one feeling.

So Step 0 is not "pick a genre and make it cool." It is:

> **Name one specific feeling. Then make the core verb BE that feeling.**

That's the whole secret. When the thing you do most often *is* the feeling —
not a delivery mechanism for it — the game has a soul. When the verb is
generic and the feeling is bolted on as theme/story, it's a tech demo wearing
a costume.

## The test that actually matters

Not "would a stranger click play." Not "is it cool to a 14-year-old."
Those questions produce generic spectacle — they would have killed every
quiet, specific, *felt* game ever made. The real test is harsher:

> **Can you name the feeling in one phrase, and does the core verb embody it?**

| Feeling (one phrase) | Core verb that IS the feeling |
|---|---|
| The ache of an unfinished promise | walk on, while your clockwork heart winds down |
| Lonely competence in a hostile, beautiful wild | survive a world that is gorgeous AND wants you dead |
| The held breath before everything goes wrong | defuse, knowing one wrong cut ends it |
| The joy of a body that moves perfectly | flow through a space where momentum is everything |
| Tiny against the vast | be small, in a frame dominated by something enormous |

If you can't fill in the left column in one phrase, you don't have a game
yet — you have a genre. Go back.

If the left column is filled but the right column is a *generic* verb that
doesn't embody it ("collect coins", "shoot enemies"), the feeling is a
costume. Either change the verb so it carries the feeling, or change the
feeling to one this verb actually delivers.

## Three quick gut-checks (seconds, not gates)

These keep a real feeling from collapsing into vagueness. They are *not* a
pass/fail rubric — they're how you smell whether the feeling is sharp enough
to build toward.

1. **One arresting frame.** Picture the single most representative moment.
   Is it a *specific image* you can see — dusk, a small brass figure, the
   sea ahead? Or is it a vague mood ("a peaceful forest")? A feeling you
   can't picture is a feeling you can't build. Vague is the enemy, not quiet.
   (Quiet + specific is how Journey, ABZÛ, Gris all work.)

2. **The verb survives stripping.** Take away score, goal, story, enemies.
   Just the verb in an empty space for 10 seconds. Does it still carry a
   hint of the feeling? Mario's jump feels good naked. A walk that winds
   down a clock has a faint dread even with nothing around it. If the bare
   verb is nothing, the feeling is living entirely in the decoration — fix
   the verb.

3. **You could describe it to one specific person and they'd lean in.**
   Not "a stranger in a Steam lineup" (that rewards loud and generic). One
   person who likes the *kind* of thing this is. If even they wouldn't lean
   in, the feeling isn't specific enough yet — or you don't believe in it,
   which is worse.

## Form is a friend, not a cage

This engine is **pure code, a few hundred lines, one screen-ish of world.**
You will not make Breath of the Wild. Good — that's the constraint that makes
you find a style. Like the GameBoy's 4 colors forced Tetris and Link's
Awakening, "pure code, short, single-mechanic" forces you toward the thing
this medium is *great* at:

- a **short arc** with a beginning and an end, not an endless sandbox
- **one mechanic**, deep enough to carry the feeling, not five shallow ones
- **strong, specific mood** over broad content
- a **few narrative beats** that land, not a script

Aim at *that* sweet spot and you can make something that moves people. Aim at
"a big game" and it falls apart into a thin everything. `clockheart` is 332
lines and it's the most alive thing in the gallery. That's not a coincidence —
it did one feeling, made the verb carry it, and stopped.

## Make the feeling, then subtract

Once you have the feeling and the verb that embodies it, **everything you add
must serve that feeling, and everything that doesn't, you cut.** This is the
discipline that turns a concept into a work. A second mechanic that's fun but
dilutes the feeling? Cut it. A gorgeous set-piece that's off-tone? Cut it.
The work is in the subtraction as much as the building.

---

## After you have the feeling

Write it down at the top of your `<game>.design.md`:

1. **The feeling** — one phrase.
2. **The core verb, and why doing it IS that feeling** — one sentence.
3. **The one arresting frame** — one sentence you can see.
4. **The ending beat** — what the player should feel in the last 10 seconds.
   (Decide the ending early. A work knows where it's going.)

Then go to `workflow.md`.

> Note on history: an earlier version of this doc optimized for "would a
> stranger click / is it cool to a teen / default to bright neon spectacle."
> That doctrine produces lively-looking, soulless games — and would have
> rejected the one real work in this gallery. Spectacle is one valid feeling
> among many ("the joy of destruction" is real), but it is not the goal.
> A specific feeling, fully realized, is the goal.
